\begin{problem}
  Consider the rigid simple pendulum:
  \begin{align}
    \frac{\dd\omega}{\dd t} &= -\frac{g}{\ell}\,\sin\theta \\
    \frac{\dd\theta}{\dd t} &= \omega ~,
  \end{align}
  where $m,\ell,g$ are constants with units of $\kg$, $\m$, and $\mps$.
  Use your leapfrog integrator to integrate these through full orbits (or a little bit more).
  Plot phase space $\theta, \omega$ orbits for a set of initial conditions
  that start at initial conditions with $\theta_0=0$ and $\omega_0$ in the list
  \newcommand{\foo}{\sqrt{\frac{g}{\ell}}}
  \begin{align}
    \omega_0 &\in \left\{0.2\,\foo, 0.4\,\foo, 0.6\,\foo, \cdots, 4.0\,\foo \right\} ~.
  \end{align}
  Plot your plot in the range $-2\,\pi<\theta<2\,\pi$ and $-2.5\,\sqrt{g/\ell}<\omega<2.5\,\sqrt{g/\ell}$.
  You should have some curves that are ``closed orbits'' and some that are ``open orbits.''
  Think hard about how you repeat your curves with offsets of $\pm2\,\pi$ to properly fill the space.
\end{problem}

\begin{note}
  What step size should you use for this problem?
  Well, the natural timescale is $\sqrt{l/g}$, so probably $0.01\,\sqrt{\ell/g}$ is probably good enough for our purposes.
\end{note}
  
\begin{note}
  You might want to only integrate for ``quarter orbits'' and then use reflection symmetry to fill out the plot.
  After all, the problem is symmetric around $\theta=0$ and around $\omega=0$.
  So, in principle, you can integrate each trajectory until either $\omega$ goes negative or else $\theta$ goes past $\pi$, and then do a set of reflections around $\theta=0$, $\omega=0$, and both.
  If you plot all possible reflections, plus offsets by $\pm 2\,\pi$, you will fill out the phase space.
\end{note}

\begin{problem}
  You can think of an ODE as a map:
  You start with initial conditions $p,q$ and you integrate forward in time to get a new state $p,q$.
  That's a mapping $p,q\rightarrow p,q$.
  The simplest (maybe) interesting map is the one-dimensional \emph{logistic map}:
  \begin{align}
    x &\leftarrow r\,x\,[1 - x] ~,
  \end{align}
  where $r$ is a continuous parameter setting how strong the nonlinearity is.

  Write simple code that iterates this map.
  At every $r$ value in \code{np.arange(2.5, 4.0, 0.001)} do the following:
  Starting at $x=0.5$, iterate the map 1000 times as a ``burn-in phase'' at one fixed value of $r$.
  Then make a scatter plot of $x$ \foreign{vs} $r$ for the next 100 iterations.
  Then move to the next $r$ value.

  Look up this map on the internets.
  What does it have to do with chaos?
\end{problem}

\begin{note}
  Play around with your plotting so that you can see all the amazing structure.
  You will want to make your points tiny!
\end{note}

\begin{note}
  Most values of $r$ produce, on this plot, one fixed point, or 2, or $2^n$, or none.
  The chaotic transitions happen when triples of fixed points appear.
  Can you find an $r$ value that has a triplet of fixed points?
  That's called a 3-cycle, and it is critical to the onset of chaos.
\end{note}

\begin{note}
  Hogg speaks of ``moving to the next $r$ value'' but if you are clever,
  you can do all $r$ values in your list all at once, in parallel.
  If you want to think about how to code, figure out how to do this problem
  with only one for loop (the loop over iterations of the map, which requires
  sequential execution).
\end{note}

\begin{problem}
  Consider the three-dimensional dynamical system
  \begin{align}
    H &= \frac{|\vec{p}|^2}{2\,m} - \frac{G\,M\,m}{|\vec{r}|} + \Omega^2\,m\,[x^2 + y^2 - 2\,z^2] ~,
  \end{align}
  where $H$ is the Hamiltonian,
  $\vec{p}$ is the non-relativistic 2-momentum,
  $\vec{r}=x\,\hat{x}+y\,\hat{y}+z\,\hat{z}$ is the 2-displacement from the origin,
  the particle mass $m\ll M$ is tiny,
  and the dynamics are precisely Hamiltonian.
  Work in units of $\au$, Solar masses, and $\yr$, and work with a $M$ equal to one Solar mass and consider orbits that are about $1\,\au$ away from the origin.
  We are going to integrate orbits in this potential, setting $\Omega^2$ to something small but signficant.

  \subpart Consider only initial conditions that start at
  \begin{align}
    \vec{r}_0 = [x_0,y_0,z_0]^\top &= [1\,\au, 0, 0]^\top \\
    \vec{v}_0 = [v_{x0},v_{y0},v_{z0}]^\top &= [v_{x0},v_\mathrm{circ},v_{z0}]^\top \,
  \end{align}
  where $v_\mathrm{circ}$ is the circular velocity ($2\,\pi\,\au\,\yr^{-1}$) at $1\,\au$ (when $\Omega^2=0$).
  We are going to arbitrarily fix $\Omega^2=0.5$ (in these units) and arbitrarily fix $v_{z0}=0.6\,\au\,\yr^{-1}$ such that there is only one free parameter, that is, $v_{x0}$.
  Write code that can integrate this system with leapfrog with a step-size that is (say) $10^{-4}\,\yr$, and integrate for something like 200 years.
  Make your code save the positions and the velocities and the corresponding times (and remember that the positions and the velocities are at different times when you use leapfrog).
  Make a plot of an orbit in the space of $x$ \foreign{vs} $z$ like the one Prof Hogg put on Discord(tm).
  Check that your code is correct by setting $\Omega^2=0$ and checking that your orbits are closed ellipses, in both position space and velocity space.
  That is, also plot the position and velocity slices of phase space for some particular orbits at $\Omega^2=0$.

  \subpart We are now going to look at a \emph{surface of section}.
  We make this by looking for times that the orbit passes through $z=0$ with $v_z>0$.
  For each such passage, use linear interpolation (or equivalent) on your leapfrog outputs to figure out the precise time $t_\text{surf}$ at which the system passed through $z=0$.
  Now interpolate the discretized positions to that time, and the discretized velocities also to that time, to get $\vec{r}_\text{surf}$ and $\vec{v}_\text{surf}$.
  Check your code by setting $\Omega^2=0$ and checking that they are perfectly periodic (or close enough, given your finite step size).
  Note that the two interpolations are in general different, because the positions and velocities in leapfrog are on different time grids.
  Once all this interpolation is working, set $\Omega^2=0.5$ and make scatter plots of $\rho_\text{surf},v_{\rho,\text{surf}}$ for some different values of $v_{x0}$, where these coordinates are defined in the cylindrical coordinate system by
  \begin{align}
    \rho^2 &= x^2 + y^2 \\
    v_\rho &= \frac{1}{\rho}\,[v_x\,x + v_y\,y] ~.
  \end{align}
  When $\Omega^2=0.5$ is finite and $v_{x0}$ is small, these scatter plots should make little loops in phase space.
\end{problem}
